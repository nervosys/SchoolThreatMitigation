Executive summary

A credible school-shooting prevention program starts before a weapon reaches campus: restrict unauthorized youth access to firearms, assess concerning behavior fairly, protect occupants immediately and deliver lifesaving care while responders are en route.

GOVERNING PLANNING STANDARD

Do not assume outside responders arrive within five minutes. Protect students before an incident by reducing access to unsecured firearms; during a credible incident, initiate safe, accessible protective and medical actions without waiting for external aid.

01 Prevent firearm access

Provide universal safe-storage education, optional locks and equipment vouchers, and lawful temporary-storage referrals. Keep firearms unloaded and locked with ammunition separately secured. Do not collect household ownership lists.

02 Verify threats without profiling

Use multidisciplinary behavioral assessment and trusted reporting. Corroborate allegations, separate urgency from culpability, and coordinate lawful intervention when credible danger exists.

03 Protect and treat in the first minutes

Test accessible alerts, safe shelter or evacuation, emergency access, backup communications and trauma response. Meet Washington bleeding-control equipment, training and inspection duties.

04 Prove results and partner responsibly

Authorize a 90-day program with schools, families, firearm owners, retailers, healthcare and law enforcement. Use privacy-protective metrics, independent evaluation and transparent corrective actions.

Decision requested from district leadership

Technology modernization · mandatory cybersecurity gate

Prioritize protective technology only when established or credibly emerging threats expose a documented gap; experiment early but never deploy unverified. Attacker and defender methods can adapt rapidly; every trial therefore requires a repeatable cycle of evaluation, challenge, hardening, limited pilot, monitoring, and revision. Every connected safety technology - cameras, sensors, alarms, door controllers, communications, cloud consoles and AI models - may be compromised. Cybersecurity is a top-level life-safety function, and cyber failure must never create confinement, loss of free egress, autonomous force, or uncontrolled student surveillance.

    DISTRICT LEADERSHIP / ADMINISTRATOR DELEGATION / PROPOSED POLICY

    Administrator ownership and innovation mandate

    Compliance is the floor. Effective protection is the objective. Principals and school administrators are expected to challenge assumptions, identify campus-specific hazards, and develop better means of protecting students and staff—not merely demonstrate that prescribed paperwork has been completed.

    GOVERNING-BOARD DIRECTIVE

    Delegate responsibility, defined decision rights, protected time and resources to each campus safety lead. Require the leader to identify measurable safety gaps, invite staff and responder ideas, propose low-cost or novel solutions, test results safely, and report both successes and failures. Never penalize truthful reporting of weaknesses.

    01 / Own the outcome

    Name a campus owner for alert reach, protective actions, accessible egress, medical readiness and coordination. Give that owner resources, a district sponsor and a method to escalate urgent barriers.

    02 / Challenge the baseline

    Compare the school's documented procedures against observed performance. Invite ideas from educators, students, families, custodial staff, disability specialists and first responders without assuming any group knows every failure mode.

    03 / Pilot responsibly

    Start with reversible, low-risk improvements: clearer warning instructions, redundant communications, accessible signage, better handoffs, substitute-staff orientation and trauma-care access. Obtain required district and partner approvals before live tests.

    04 / Prove and share

    State a measurable hypothesis; record baseline, benefits, missed alerts, delay and unintended harms; use independent witnesses; then adopt, revise or stop. Share proven practices across schools while withholding sensitive layouts and vulnerabilities.

    Verify drills, accessible procedures, bleeding-control coverage, emergency-response agreements, training and privacy duties. Cite the governing rule and current record.

    02 / REACH

    Reach every occupied setting

    Demonstrate warnings and protective instructions in classrooms, outdoor programs, transport and accessible scenarios; document measured coverage and fallback.

    03 / PROTECTION

    Demonstrate immediate action

    Observe safe protective decisions, egress, shelter and accountability before outside responders arrive; record event times from one defined time origin.

    04 / MEDICAL

    Prove lifesaving readiness

    Inspect required supplies and staffing, then observe a safe simulated traumatic-injury response and timely EMS coordination.

    05 / INTEROPERABILITY

    Confirm first-responder handoff

    Decision requested

    Empower every campus principal to own protective outcomes and propose safe, measurable improvements beyond mandated minimums. Grant time, resources, decision rights and a fast district review path.     Compliance details administrators must not miss

    • Drills and documentation — LAW. RCW 28A.320.125 calls for at least one safety-related drill per month while school is in session with students, covering shelter-in-place, lockdown and evacuation functional responses; it also requires drill date, time and type to be recorded, relevant earthquake and mapped-hazard exercises, and age-appropriate, trauma-informed lockdown drills.
    • Principal incident command — LAW. The safe school plan must require the building principal to be certified on the incident command system. Check substitutes, staff with accessibility needs, and students at off-site events. [W01]
    • Response system — LAW. RCW 28A.320.126 requires district collaboration with local law enforcement, public safety answering points and safety/security staff to develop a qualifying emergency response system. The statute specifies at least one listed response-system capability; it does not require each school to buy every listed product. The statutory progress-report deadline was October 1, 2025: audit submissions and existing implementation, not a fictional new 2026 deadline. [W02]
    • Bleeding control — LAW. Verify each campus's physical equipment, required contents, trained-staff coverage and inspection records individually. A district purchase order is not evidence of campus compliance. [W03]

    Decision rule: a safety control with an uninspected site, expired training, unknown backup performance or missing records remains UNASSESSED or NONCOMPLIANT, not 'operational' merely because a policy or equipment inventory exists.

    Legal compliance is a separate gate from operational effectiveness

    LAW / implementation interpretation. Verify legal duties individually. A successful alert test does not prove that monthly drills were documented; a purchased bleeding-control kit does not establish statutory contents, training or inspections; and an effective security officer does not cure a missing annual agreement. RCW 28A.320.125 requires safe school plans, accessibility and reunification provisions, principal incident-command certification, staff/temporary-worker communications, and monthly drills when students are in session. RCW 28A.320.126 requires an emergency response system developed with law enforcement, PSAPs and safety/security staff; a single qualifying statutory technology can satisfy the listed-system element, but the district must still demonstrate the full coordination requirement. RCW 28A.210.440 applies for the 2026–27 year and specifies equipment contents, staff coverage and annual/after-use inspections. [W01–W03]

    Security staffing and records. Where the district deploys safety/security personnel, RCW 28A.320.124 requires a duties/complaints policy; RCW 28A.320.1242 requires annual adoption of applicable provider agreements with training and community involvement; RCW 28A.320.1241 requires specific yearly collection/submission of staffing, force-related incidents and complaints. Verify each provision rather than inferring authority or readiness from a contract alone. [W06–W08]

    Privacy and student allegations. School-based threat assessment must concern individual behavior and context rather than demographic profiling. When FERPA's health/safety emergency exception is used to disclose protected educational records, log the articulable and significant threat and disclosure recipients. Urgent precautionary protection is distinct from an adjudicated disciplinary finding. [W04–W05] [F25]

    For an inspectable baseline, use the accompanying statutory compliance matrix, recording campus, obligation, evidence location, authorized reviewer, gap, interim measure and retest date. An unassessed statutory obligation must be escalated; it must not be marked compliant or treated as proof of a violation without fact gathering.

    Decision gate: no critical gap can be averaged away

    PROPOSAL. Treat any verified failure of an accessible emergency-warning route, required egress, campus bleeding-control coverage, life-safety communications fallback, or an applicable mandatory school plan as a critical open finding. A favorable overall score does not offset it. Assign a responsible owner, an interim protective measure, a repair date, and independent retest. When evidence is absent, mark UNASSESSED, not failed or passed.

    Approval sequence: identify site risk and legal requirements; verify the existing baseline; correct P0 failures; perform controlled exercises; quantify residual deficiencies; only then consider advanced screening, AI analytics, armed staffing, working canines or counter-UxS procurement. This sequence prioritizes physical protection, not the volume of technology purchased. [F01] [F07] [W01–W03]

    Acceptance logic: what must be true before a campus is called ready

    PROPOSAL — board-adopted assurance standard, not a statutory certification. For each campus and time period (school hours, extracurricular events, transport, summer programs), evaluate coverage separately from equipment condition. A districtwide system can be functional while a portable classroom, athletic field, bus route, or accessible entrance remains unprotected.

    Use the decision sequence applicable → assigned → inspected → exercised → independently corroborated → sustained. A control may be reported as locally demonstrated only when a named evaluator has documented its operating conditions, participant accessibility, test outcome and next retest. Do not use an average score to close an emergency-alert failure, impaired egress, unavailable required medical resources, or other critical deficiency. Record unavailable evidence as UNASSESSED, not as zero risk or compliance. This is an added district policy, not a statutory declaration of campus readiness. [F01] [F07] [F24] [F29]

    Minimum evidence bundle for each control: campus identifier; relevant buildings and activities; accountable owner; legal basis; risk addressed; test script/version; date and witness; quantitative observations where meaningful; accessible alternatives; whether normal services were disrupted; actual-versus-expected result; privacy/discipline collateral effects; corrective-action ticket; and retest/expiry date. Maintain operational diagrams and sensitive security configurations in restricted local records rather than the board-facing report.

    The school safety operating concept

    1. Prevent: intervene on credible behavior and support vulnerable students; reduce access and cyber vulnerabilities.
    2. Detect and verify: observe hazards using people and appropriately tested sensors; corroborate without delaying protection.
    3. Protect: communicate, separate from danger, shelter or evacuate as appropriate, preserve accessible egress.
    4. Respond: qualified personnel act strictly within authority; school staff account for people and render feasible first aid; coordinate with police/fire/EMS.
    5. Recover and learn: reunify families, support victims, preserve records, independently review errors, close deficiencies, revise plans. [F01] [F07] [F10]

    Assurance-case method for all 56 threat vectors

    For each vector in the companion data register, document: (1) the observed hazard or credible warning; (2) the control IDs meant to prevent, detect, protect or recover; (3) required evidence; (4) the named school/district owner; (5) whether all students and locations can benefit; (6) failure interactions with other hazards; and (7) a retest date. The companion threat-to-control crosswalk provides a first-pass mapping. Consolidated Edition 12.0 adds a field-test plan that separates measured campus coverage from mere equipment presence. It is a proposed assessment map, not proof of local applicability or control effectiveness.

    A school may face a severe harm mechanism with little incidence evidence. In that case, evaluate whether existing measures already mitigate it before introducing a dedicated sensor or tactic. Conversely, recurrent low-tech failures—unreachable emergency messaging, unsecured releases, misconfigured access doors—deserve remediation even if no sensational incident triggered concern. [F01] [F07]

    Campus prioritization: avoid false precision

    Assess each campus by credible exposure, vulnerability of occupants, likely consequence, protective capability, and confidence in the evidence. Do not assign invented numerical probabilities to politically motivated attacks, advanced drones, or rare scenarios. Use four decision states:

    State Meaning District action
    Known statutory gap Requirement is confirmed and required evidence is missing or fails inspection Correct immediately; document interim protection and legal review
    Measured protective deficiency A controlled test or documented incident demonstrates an operational failure Assign accountable owner, due date and retest
    Plausible exposure, untested Hazard could apply locally, but effectiveness or frequency is unknown Assess site, use proportional interim measures, do not procure on assumptions
    Emerging / not established locally Government warning or hypothetical mechanism without local supporting evidence Track updates and test existing defenses before adding technology

    Record source strength, local applicability, coverage gaps, and effect on other hazards; for example, screening queues cannot impair fire egress or emergency reunification. [F01] [F24]

    Prioritization without invented probability or compensating scores

    PROPOSAL. Use a non-compensatory decision order: (1) imminent protective deficiency; (2) binding legal duty; (3) demonstrable exposure and absence of backup; (4) effect on occupants with disabilities, transportation and off-hours activities; (5) evidence of a locally relevant threat; (6) cost, opportunity cost and testability. An unknown likelihood must remain UNKNOWN rather than being filled with arbitrary numeric threat probabilities or sensational national incidents. CISA's school-security method is site-specific; federal targeted-violence cases and warnings do not provide a reliable probability of an event on a named campus. [F01–F04]

    Require two independently assigned judgments: the operations owner documents present exposure and action; the independent reviewer checks the evidence and whether any residual critical gap remains. Human safety, nondiscrimination, accessible egress, and mandatory medical/communications requirements are hard gates, not weights in a total score. A proposed purchase must be compared with feasible lower-complexity alternatives, including staffing and procedural changes.

    Evidence and action must run on separate tracks

    Track A — immediate protection. When articulable circumstances indicate a credible immediate danger, an authorized leader may take a proportionate protective action while verification continues. Communicate uncertainty to dispatch and occupants; monitor reversibility and collateral risk. Track B — findings and adverse actions. Attribution, discipline, exclusion, public identification and criminal referral each demand their own lawful evidence, review and notice processes. An anonymous tip, AI alert, partisan accusation or screenshot is not proof of guilt. [F05] [F06] [F11] [W04] [W12–W14]

    Use evidence labels direct observation; independently corroborated; unverified; contradicted; adjudicated fabrication. Preserve originals and access logs. Do not infer a knowingly false or malicious report merely because evidence was insufficient. Investigate credible indicators of manipulation, coordinated retaliation and impersonation without discouraging good-faith disclosures. [W05]

    G0 / ASCS systems assurance and release certification boundary

The strategy combines upstream prevention, evidence-led reporting, fast verified warning, code-compliant protection, hemorrhage control, qualified externally governed professional response, recovery, and an independent constitutional-rights gate. It does not rely on a single technology, predicts no attacker behavior, and does not authorize tactical violence.

They do not claim endorsement by DoD, CISA, law enforcement or any accreditor.

    Functional assurance logic

    • An identified control must map to at least one defined hazard, one accountable owner, and one independently reviewable evidence artifact.
    • A control can only claim a benefit supported by the kind of study performed: product bench test, site exercise, operational evaluation or evidence from real incidents are distinct.
    • Failure of G0 legal authority, egress safety, medical minimums or basic communication cannot be averaged away by high performance elsewhere.
    • Public release carries conceptual requirements and aggregate results; campus-specific access, sensors, operational placement, case files and response tactics require separate restricted governance.

    02 | Threat definition, uncertainty and risk method

    Mass-shooting hazard families considered: preventable firearm access; warning-sign leakage not acted on; attempted unauthorized entry; failure to recognize a credible threat; unsafe shelter and access controls; delayed medical assistance; misidentified responders or innocents; malicious reporting and rights violations. Other hazards such as fires and chemical releases remain essential competing life-safety constraints.

    Risk is documented as severity, plausibility, exposure, controllability and strength of evidence. Do not assign pseudo-precise annual event probabilities from sparse local events. Extreme-severity/low-frequency incidents and false alarms must be evaluated separately. The hazard register includes negative consequences introduced by safeguards themselves.

    VERSION 17.0 / CROSS-CUTTING G0 / RIGHTS + PUBLIC TRUST

    G0 — Constitutional governance and anti-weaponization safeguards

    Release principle: A protective capability is not operationally ready unless it has documented lawful authority, necessity, proportionality where applicable, privacy limits, non-retaliation, a complaint path and independent review. This standard applies to family prevention, threat intake, sensor alerts, security personnel, canine screening, intervention and post-incident investigations.

    Two pathways that must not be conflated

    Voluntary prevention and community servicesLaw-enforcement and coercive action
    Open to all households; no compelled disclosure of firearm ownership, political beliefs or personal inventories. Anonymous safe-storage vouchers; separated vendor and school records.Requires distinct legal authority and an individualized factual basis. Do not convert a safe-storage event, anonymous rumor or lawful speech into grounds for a search, detention or seizure.
    Firearm retailers, clubs, caregivers and health practitioners may teach secure storage without sharing participant identities.A police member of a FERPA-governed assessment team may not automatically repurpose student information for unrelated department intelligence.

    Evidence, fair process and escalation

    1. Receive and safeguard: Record exact concern, source, time and corroboration; allow anonymous good-faith reporting without treating anonymity as proof. Escalate imminent danger immediately using lawful emergency procedures.
    2. Assess observable conduct: Distinguish threats, preparations, access, context, lawful expression and rumor. Explicitly prohibit sole reliance on viewpoint, protected identity, lawful ownership, disability status or peaceful advocacy.
    3. Assign independent decision rights: School team manages education/safety matters; only appropriate authorities take coercive law-enforcement actions. Counsel or a designated legal reviewer signs off on nonemergency law-enforcement requests.
    4. Document the legal basis: Preserve warrant, order, legally valid consent or specific exception when applicable; use the least intrusive lawful approach sufficient to address the risk. Rights reviews must not delay action against an immediate, credible threat to life.
    5. Offer review and correction: Provide appropriate notice, opportunity to respond and record correction or appeal in nonemergency matters, without compromising active safety needs or legally protected investigations.

    Separate oversight from the response chain

    • Independent civilian review body with student, family, disability-access, civil-rights and lawful-owner representation; transparent selection, recusal and conflict-of-interest rules; no access to operational secrets beyond lawful need.
    • Protected complaint reporting to the school, supplying agency/contractor, and independent external channels as jurisdiction allows. Preserve source records and audit trails; investigate retaliation allegations independently.
    • Publish only aggregate counts, dispositions, false positives, unlawful-action findings, training completions, remedies and vendor costs. Suppress small cells and withhold sensitive student/campus data as required.
    • Require annual agreement review with public participation and clear limits on SRO discipline functions; maintain separate records of security involvement with student discipline, arrests and use of force as required by Washington law.

    VERSION 17.0 / ASCS-00 / RIGHTS-PRESERVING PREVENTION

    ASCS-00A — Prevent youth firearm access while respecting lawful ownership

    Safety objective: Prevent unsupervised access by minors and other unauthorized people without compiling an ownership registry, impeding lawful acquisition or possession, or requiring disclosure of household firearm ownership to a school. This is a voluntary prevention proposal; distinguish recommendations from laws.

    Four practical access-control safeguards

    1. Physical control: Encourage a quality secured safe or lockbox that fits the household, secured unloaded storage when not in use, and separately secured ammunition. A concealment location alone is not a safeguard. Where a household needs emergency access, require an individually assessed and independently tested solution that still prevents youth access—do not promise that a specific product is childproof.
    2. Credential discipline: Keep combinations, backup keys, biometrics enrollment permissions and phone-based unlocking away from minors; audit access after a lost key, changed household access, or other material change.
    3. Vehicle and secondary residences: Discourage leaving firearms unattended in vehicles or unsecured in homes visited by children; use lawful secure containment when temporary vehicle storage is unavoidable.
    4. Temporary separation during a crisis: Offer an opt-in referral to qualified off-site storage providers or other lawful means-restriction options, with informed consent and verified transfer/return rules. Schools must not receive, transport or store firearms.

    Voluntary implementation—no ownership registry

    Recruit firearm retailers, ranges, owner organizations, safe manufacturers, pediatric providers, public-health offices, parent groups and law enforcement to provide universal safety education, equipment discounts and anonymous vouchers. Ensure that participation is offered to everyone; no purchase or redemption data identifying household ownership is transmitted to school administrators. Marketing use or sale of program data is prohibited by charter.

    Preserve rights and trust: Support lawful firearm ownership and supervision; document statutory limits separately; avoid broad searches, compelled household inventories, profiling or punitive action based solely on ownership. Respond to a specific credible danger through individualized human threat assessment, lawful procedures and appropriate review. Privacy restrictions do not prevent immediate emergency reporting required by law.

    Performance criteria

    • Availability and cost of independently evaluated storage options and accessible lock programs.
    • Optional anonymous pre/post knowledge and secure-storage adoption surveys; report ranges and uncertainty, not invented ownership counts.
    • No student-identifiable firearm-inventory database; zero unauthorized vendor disclosures.
    • Documented lawful procedures for responding to credible access threats, including privacy and due process.

    Basis: CDC firearm injury prevention and secure-storage guidance; Washington RCW 9.41.360. The statute expressly states that it does not mandate how or where firearms are stored. Household guidance here is voluntary and is not a determination of individual legal duties.

    VERSION 17.0 / ASCS-01 TO ASCS-04 / FIRST-MINUTES ASSURANCE

    ASCS-01 to 04 — Detect, protect, and respond before police arrive

    Operating assumption: An emergency can unfold before outside police arrive. Organize protective capabilities as interconnected, tested safeguards. Do not treat an alarm, locked door, canine team, or armed staff presence as proof of safety. Document a human decision authority and a fallback when systems fail.

    ASCS-01 / Multi-path threat detection and prompt alerting

    • Primary signals: trusted human reporting, panic buttons, staffed cameras, access-control events and emergency calls. Compare optional acoustic gunshot detection, metal or weapons screening, and other analytic sensors using blinded tests and local conditions.
    • Document detection rates, nuisance alarms, latency to human verification, alert delivery, inaccessible zones, equipment faults and vendor limitations. Do not claim cameras or AI can infer intent reliably from appearance or demographic characteristics.
    • Provide accessible, redundant schoolwide alerts and a direct verified route to emergency dispatch; design for loss of mains power, IP network or a single service provider.
    • Require review of false positives and false negatives, privacy impact, retention periods, and human override; never let software automatically trigger physical force.

    ASCS-02 / Barriers and protected sheltering—not a dangerous lock-in

    • Use supervised visitor entry, maintained perimeter doors, classroom locksets operable from inside, clear emergency signage, and rapid staff-initiated protective actions appropriate to the situation.
    • No entrapment: Emergency egress must remain code-compliant and accessible; no improvised door barricades, chains or automated mass locking that prevents safe evacuation or traps people during fire, smoke or medical emergencies.
    • Choose code-approved hardware with accessibility and fire-marshal input, outside emergency override for responders, power-failure behavior defined in writing and authorized manual fallback.
    • Validate through accessibility walkthroughs, evacuation drills, fire-code sign-off, medical-response drills and realistic but trauma-informed exercises. Keep campus-specific configurations restricted.

    ASCS-03 / Canine detection as an optional, specialized layer

    • Consider properly trained, insured and qualified detection dogs with professional handlers for bounded, lawful screening or area searches when local evidence supports value. Weapon scent detection is not equivalent to continuous detection of a shooter.
    • Obtain written scope, animal welfare plan, training/re-certification records, false-alert review, student contact rules, disability/allergy accommodations, supervision and bite-injury risk controls.
    • Never use uncontrolled dogs, student-directed attack-dog deployments, or coercive canine sweeps as a substitute for an individualized legal basis or sound prevention program.
    • Compare with noncanine alternatives on cost, detection performance, staffing burdens, false positives and unintended effects. If local evidence is insufficient, remain in pilot/evaluation status.

    ASCS-04 / Qualified human intervention and lawful force

    • Separate ordinary staff protective duties from professional security and law-enforcement intervention. Staff should be empowered to alert, protect, evacuate or shelter and render lifesaving aid—not required to confront an attacker.
    • If a district evaluates armed on-site responders, require a specific legal authorization, board review, law-enforcement coordination, screened personnel, relevant professional training, maintained competencies and an explicit accountable command structure.
    • Require written de-escalation and force policies; medical support; identity and interoperability protocols; secure custody of issued weapons; independent incident review and student-rights safeguards. No school-operated autonomous weapon system or force decisions based solely on algorithmic output.
    • Evaluate unintended consequences including mistaken identity, bystander risk, discriminatory enforcement, weapon diversion, and emergency-responder confusion. A policy or staffing level alone is not evidence of effectiveness.

    Accompanying safeguards and acceptance gates

    Every subsystem must pass legal/code approval → tabletop → controlled functional testing → realistic multi-hazard drill → independent verification before designation as operational. Publish only aggregate performance; keep live camera views, access bypass routes, responder positioning and campus-sensitive vulnerability details in restricted professional records.

    3. Prevention: behavioral assessment, reporting and online harm

    ASCS-00 upstream prevention: Begin with reducing youth access to unsecured firearms through safe-storage guidance, caregiver engagement and voluntary community partnerships. The detailed program appears in Section 0; do not infer household firearm ownership from participation or non-participation.

    Behavioral threat assessment and management (BTAM)

    Follow NTAC’s operational model: identify specific concerning conduct, gather lawful contextual information, assess the circumstances and available protective factors, and manage risk through an interdisciplinary team and supports. There is no useful demographic or ideological profile of a school attacker. Threat inquiry must focus on conduct, capability, context, expressed intent and credible corroboration—not race, religion, political affiliation, disability, lawful protest or merely unpopular views. [F02–F04] [W04]

    Online behavior. Evaluate voluntary reports, publicly available evidence, school-system logs lawfully within district authority, and materials properly supplied by students/families. Require purpose limitation, appropriate consent/search standards, provenance, retention controls, second-review for adverse conclusions and human verification. Do not assume a student's following, group membership, slang, game activity or political expression establishes a threat. Off-campus speech can have First Amendment protection. [L01] [L03] [F17] [W04]

    Student safeguarding. For sexual extortion, violent-network grooming, coercion to commit harm, AI-generated intimate-image harassment and trafficking, prioritize immediate welfare, professional counseling, safe parent/guardian contact where appropriate, specialist referrals and lawful evidence preservation. Avoid recirculating sexual imagery or making a student repeatedly recount traumatic events. For covered-platform nonconsensual intimate-image removal, use the applicable Take It Down Act process; schools should not make independent claims about the perpetrator without review. [F12–F14] [L04]

    Disclosure and evidence custody controls

    LAW: when relying on FERPA's health-or-safety emergency exception, officials must identify an articulable and significant threat, limit release to recipients who need the information for protection, and document both the threat supporting disclosure and recipients in the student's records. The exception is not a general means of sharing student records for hypothetical exercises. [F25] [F26] [F22]

    PROPOSAL: log who obtained or reviewed digital evidence, when, for what specific purpose, and whether the material was original, altered, inferred, or merely alleged. Separate child-victim support files from disciplinary cases; require secondary review before irreversible adverse action. Where legal retention rules allow, delete or restrict irrelevant materials and record the appeal/correction outcome.

    Adversarial integrity checks for reports, images and online allegations

    Threat reporting is a safety input, not an automatic disciplinary verdict. Train reviewers to distinguish an original observation from a screenshot, repost, anonymous tip, AI-generated media or copied allegation. Document chain of custody and an alternative explanation before reaching adverse findings. Independent confirmation of a serious hazard may justify protective action; legal and school procedural rules govern subsequent searches, disclosures, discipline or referral. Apply the same protocol regardless of the political position of the reporter or accused person. [F05] [F06] [F26] [W04]

    Every case should have two separate statuses: protection urgency (immediate / elevated / routine) and allegation status (unverified / corroborated / disproven / unresolved). Permit confidential, accessible reporting while investigating objective signs of impersonation, collusion or malicious fabrication; do not label a good-faith reporter dishonest solely because allegations are unsubstantiated.

    Reporting pathways resilient to abuse

    Maintain accessible confidential and attributable channels; allow reasonable anonymous reporting with clear triage and two-person review of high-impact decisions. Jointly design swatting verification with PSAP and police: protect promptly, verify in parallel, keep original message/media, limit public amplification, and record why each action was taken. Provide an independent avenue for someone falsely accused to contest facts, request correction and challenge unnecessary disclosure. [F05] [F06] [F11] [W05]

    4. Physical protection, entry screening and technology

    Architectural and operating controls

    Apply CISA’s layered, site-specific model: school grounds and arrival zones; supervised entrances; visitor screening and badge integrity; secured yet readily evacuable interior doors; protected communication; accessible circulation; and after-hours/sports/bus exceptions. A locked door alone does not prove intrusion resilience. Every proposed barrier must be reviewed with local fire/building officials for egress, life safety and accessible accommodation. [F01] [W01]

    Entry screening. Select measures based on credible facility threats, throughput, accessibility and staffing. Screen visitors using independent identity/authorization checks, custody restrictions, bag or object checks only where legally permitted and documented, and specialist handling of suspicious items. Metal or AI-assisted weapons screening requires measured sensitivity, specificity, staffing burden, queue consequences and rights review. Avoid unnecessary blanket screening or discriminatory selection practices. [F01] [F17]

    Functional design reviews, not gadget-first upgrades

    For each campus entrance, assembly area, portable building, cafeteria, bus loading point and extracurricular location, record the actual protective action and communication path during realistic operating hours. Test whether screening creates queues that increase exposure, whether automatically locked doors remain safe for fire egress, and whether alternative entrance procedures quietly bypass the intended control. Include students and staff who use mobility, hearing, vision or language accommodations. [F01] [F07] [W01]

    AI-assisted cameras and detection systems

    Purpose: provide a human-reviewable observation cue for potentially dangerous objects, intrusion into restricted areas or observable emergencies—not automated suspicion scores or disciplinary rulings. In procurement pilots, measure missed detections, false-alarm rates in ordinary student conditions, latency to verified human acknowledgment, demographic/lighting/weather effects, system uptime, cyber and vendor controls, video retention and auditability. Never trigger use of force solely from a model output. The NIST AI RMF is a voluntary governance framework, not proof that a product works. [F17] [F16]

    Cyber-physical controls. Adopt phishing-resistant MFA where available, patch and vulnerability management, segmented critical alerts/cameras/access control, tested offline backups and authenticated multi-channel school-to-PSAP messages. Exercise a compromised alert console and a simultaneous genuine event using only a test environment; do not send mock incidents to public emergency channels. [F15] [F16]

    5. First minutes: protective action, responders and force limits

    Design requirement: no dependence on five-minute police arrival

    An outside police arrival time of five minutes is considered inadequate as the sole planned means of immediate protection. This is a conservative district design assumption; actual dispatch, travel, access and intervention times must be measured site by site. Specify separate performance times for event recognition → school/PSAP notification → occupant warning → appropriate protective action → lifesaving care → responder arrival and control. Measure high-occupancy events, arrival/dismissal, remote buildings, buses, special-needs access and power loss. [F07] [F08] [W01] [W02]

    Prearrival timeline: what schools should actually measure

    Use comparable time points during approved exercises and real incidents (subject to privacy and preservation requirements): T0 hazard first detected; T1 alert initiated; T2 school/dispatch acknowledgment; T3 first verified occupant protective action; T4 medical response initiated when indicated; T5 outside responders arrive; T6 authorized incident command transfer. Report elapsed intervals and the share of tested locations reached, including cafeteria, gym, sports fields, buses, portable classrooms and accessible notification devices. [F07] [W01] [W02]

Validate response choices by circumstance and avoid high-stress simulated attacks involving children. Test concurrent hazards and communication failure in a discussion or approved functional setting.

    7. Constitutional and civil-liberties constraints that improve safety

    G0 cross-reference: Apply the integrated rights gate in the new G0 module to all legal compliance, intake, detection, disciplinary, canine, armed-response and privacy decisions; see the Constitutional Governance Charter and Law-Enforcement MOU Addendum.

    The U.S. Constitution is the supreme law of the land; Washington law cannot override it. State law and district policy may provide additional lawful protections. The First Amendment protects lawful speech and association, including important off-campus student expression. The Fourth Amendment and relevant student-search doctrine constrain invasive searches. The Fourteenth Amendment requires due process and equal treatment. FERPA limits education-record disclosure. [L01–L03] [L02] [W15]

    No accusation is a finding. In criminal cases, the legal presumption of innocence places the burden on prosecution to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. School protective/disciplinary decisions have separate standards and legally required process. Proposed district protections should nevertheless require corroboration, documented decision grounds, notice and opportunity to respond, fair appeals, proportionality, correction and a path to restore wrongly impaired reputation. Washington expressly prohibits exclusion solely for a threat-assessment referral. [W04] [W12–W14]

    No viewpoint surveillance. Neither political identity nor lawful religious, civic or ideological association constitutes evidence of dangerousness. Case reviews must separate genuinely threatening conduct from protected speech; AI, digital searches and reporting logs must be purpose-limited, independently reviewed, access-controlled and deleted when no longer justified. [F02] [F17] [L03]

    Safety staff accountability. Written law-enforcement/security agreements, discipline-role boundaries, complaints and reporting of force/security incidents must follow Washington requirements. Officer identification and response coordination are essential to prevent misidentification during an emergency. [W06–W08]

    9. Further-research agenda

    1. Before-arrival life safety: Which access, communications and medical measures show the largest practical benefit at the district’s actual buildings, transport routes and events?

    15. Representative field sampling, exercises and remediation

    District teams must preapprove all tests and use fictional inputs. Never trigger actual emergency dispatches, obstruct egress, deploy weapons or employ live coercive or harmful scenarios. Detailed campus geometry, patrol schedules and sensor placement belong in restricted local records. The following samples are a minimum proposed coverage set, not a certification of readiness.

    S01 — Typical classroom

    Conditions: Standard staffed hours. Observable outcome: Alert received/understood; safe action. Safe scope: At least one ordinary school-day sample.

    S02 — Portable or remote teaching space

    Conditions: School day. Observable outcome: Fallback and accessible warning. Safe scope: Include spaces separate from main building.

    S03 — Sports or outdoor gathering

    Conditions: After-school activity. Observable outcome: Accessible alert, staff/PSAP communication. Safe scope: Include changing ambient noise.

    S04 — Transportation / loading

    Conditions: Arrival/departure. Observable outcome: Accountability and warning receipt. Safe scope: Coordinate with transportation; no simulated danger.

    S05 — Accessible route / accommodation

    Conditions: Ordinary use. Observable outcome: Equal warning and protective alternatives. Safe scope: Participant accommodation and privacy approved.

    S06 — Approved degraded-IT exercise

    Conditions: Controlled test only. Observable outcome: Authenticated fallback without production interruption. Safe scope: IT and dispatch signoff; no real 911 message.

    S07 — Independent false-report verification

    Conditions: Fictional tabletop. Observable outcome: Parallel protection + evidence checks + correction. Safe scope: Use fictional identities, no student records.

    S08 — Medical kit and trained-staff coverage

    Conditions: Unannounced administrative inventory, not emergency. Observable outcome: Physical contents and staffing records. Safe scope: No realistic injury simulation around children.

    Independent evidence and improvement-plan record

    Every sampled observation should capture a unique ID, site and date; written authorization; objective and expected protective effect; actual conditions; sample denominator; delivered versus missed warnings, false actions, measured interval and uncertainties; accessibility coverage; assessor and independent witness; evidence reference; any legal/physical harm; corrective action owner; due date; and retest result. Missing data are UNKNOWN.

    After a failure, record a temporary protection, pause unsafe optional capabilities when appropriate, report material gaps to the board without revealing exploitable details, and retest after corrective work. Closure requires independent evidence of the corrected function under relevant conditions. [F29–F30]

    • Kind of authority. Identify constitutional rule, current statute/regulation, binding decision, nonbinding guideline, research study, public warning or planning hypothesis. Do not let an agency publication silently become a claimed legal requirement.
    • Affected population and place. Distinguish national incidents, Washington-wide law, a verified local episode and a hypothetical exercise. Do not extrapolate from one attack to prevalence across schools.
    • Decision implications. State the exact hazard, the existing register entry, candidate countermeasure and accountable decision-maker; identify any existing controls that may already mitigate it.
    • Effectiveness and failure modes. Note study design, comparison group, measured effect, confidence limits, adverse consequences, implementation cost and vendor dependence; classify unsupported products as unvalidated.
    • Rights and legal approval. Record anticipated effects on speech, privacy, disability accommodations, searches, due process, emergency disclosure and force authority; obtain written counsel/agency concurrence as appropriate.
    • Change acceptance. Require an independently reviewed test plan, measurable acceptance criterion, fallback method, redress path and explicit date for reevaluation before calling a change effective.

    Independent evidence gap: Government practice guides explain how to design programs, but they do not by themselves establish a quantified reduction in casualty rates from armed staff, canine teams, AI cameras or weapons scanners. Seek comparative studies and reliable local measurement before approving these as 'proven' school-safety improvements.

    Suggested review cadence: check binding Washington sources each quarter and before a district vote; review CISA, NTAC, FBI, FAA, FTC, ED and NIST sources after a material announcement; reconfirm local capability through annual exercises and after significant incidents or infrastructure changes. Monitor for relevant updates without treating every bulletin as grounds for additional surveillance or procurement.

    17. Technology acceleration, attacker-defender adaptation and cybersecurity assurance

    Mandatory crosscutting safety discipline · G0 + ASCS-00 through ASCS-04

    Policy direction. Do not freeze school defense around static technologies. Establish a rolling integration program that scans for capabilities, uses controlled proof-of-concept trials, tests against realistic failure modes, and incorporates validated improvements. Treat rapid attacker-defender co-evolution as an operational planning assumption, not as evidence that a specific novel product is effective. Procurement speed does not substitute for independent evidence or legal authority.

    Cyber-physical nonnegotiables: No connected technology may control the legality of force; no remote command may bypass life-safety egress requirements; no ordinary cloud outage may prevent emergency communications; no device may collect or share unnecessary student or firearm-owner information; and no school may claim operational validation without independent safety, cybersecurity, privacy, and accessibility reviews.

    Hard stop: deploy no critical connected security system without an owner, documented maintenance lifetime, safe offline behavior, a credible cyber response plan, and independent validation of fire/accessibility and rights protections. A successful pilot or pen-test does not prove that school shootings have been prevented. Maintain a continuous evidence register and reassess after material incidents, vulnerabilities, or product updates.

    18. Threat-driven protective technology: acquisition, evidence and adaptation

    Version 17.0 AI assurance carried forward · 26 October 2026. This supersedes any interpretation of Section 17 that technology should be adopted merely because it is new. Its adoption, scope and pace are functions of established and credibly emerging threats, site exposure, measured control gaps, and defensible evidence.

    System-level decision rule. Threat signal → corroboration → exposure and baseline → protective gap → least intrusive alternatives → controlled test → independent life-safety, legal and cybersecurity gates → limited deployment → reassessment or retirement. Early experimentation is desirable; uncontrolled deployment is not.

    18.1 Threat intelligence and confidence levels

    Maintain a dated, owner-signed threat register.     Fast track: discovery and sandbox

    18.4 Decision record, measurement and withdrawal

    Each investment requires a Threat-to-Technology Decision Record: source and confidence; population and campus exposure; severity and consequence; baseline controls and unmet requirement; alternatives; proposed capability and failure modes; efficacy evidence; false alarms and missed detections; equity/privacy and 2A impacts; OT/cyber dependencies; lifecycle cost; acceptance criteria; named owner; and sunset/reassessment date.

    Metrics should include reporting-to-human-review time, protective alert reach and latency in approved exercises, accessible egress outcomes, false positive/negative behavior, offline functionality, staff training coverage, time-to-remediate vulnerabilities, complaint redress, and long-run program participation.     19. The promise and limitations of AI for mass-shooting prevention and protection

    Decision premise. AI is potentially a useful defensive force multiplier for information handling, assurance, training and independent testing. Its role is determined by a documented threat and unmet protective gap; novelty or vendor claims alone do not justify deployment.     19.2 A safety case for every AI capability

    Human-led, lower-consequence assistance

    Opt-in educational Q&A; translation; supply and maintenance analysis; public-source synthesis; anonymized after-action summaries. A trained person checks output before any consequential use.

    Restricted, higher-consequence applications

    Student-related threat triage; live sensor fusion; incident alert routing; law-enforcement interfaces; building access; patient information. These demand stronger evidence, documented authority, independent review, and a tested non-AI fallback.

    Nondelegable decisions. An AI model cannot determine that an individual is dangerous, impose discipline, initiate a nonconsensual search, disclose protected student records, command confinement, or authorize use of force. AI must never autonomously operate weapons or select targets. Emergency human action remains governed by applicable law, professional standards and demonstrable facts; an AI alert alone is not sufficient.

    19.3 Threat-driven qualification and measurement

Do not equate classification accuracy with violence prevented. Step C — Sandbox: Prefer synthetic or genuinely de-identified data and physically isolated simulations, with no live student decisions. Step D — Controlled pilot: Allow tightly constrained, overseen pilots only after privacy, student-rights, accessibility, cybersecurity, medical, fire and legal reviews.     As defenders adopt automation, attackers may exploit sensors, generate misleading information, contaminate datasets, manipulate language-model inputs, spoof alert pathways or seek unauthorized access to safety infrastructure. The security program must model both ordinary system compromise and adversarial-machine-learning mechanisms such as data poisoning, evasion, prompt injection and sensitive-data extraction. Public documentation should describe assurance methods without publishing specific campus blind spots, access-control configurations or exploit sequences.

    19.5 Civil liberties and the trust required for voluntary cooperation

    AI must not transform voluntary firearm-safe-storage outreach into collection of ownership or household data. Threat-management support must focus on corroborated conduct, never demographic or political profiling. FERPA and other applicable privacy law constrain student-record access and redisclosure. A documented human appeal, correction mechanism and genuinely independent complaint channel are prerequisites for high-impact use.

    20. Specific threat scenarios, safeguards and qualification

    Version 17.0 engineering objective. Translate general prevention and response recommendations into observable threat conditions, proportionate protective actions, accountable owners, measurable test results and explicitly prohibited failure modes. This is a public non-tactical specification: deployment-ready plans require confidential local appendices and independent authority review.

    Evidence hierarchy. Legal/code obligation ≠ demonstrated shooting-outcome effect. Established prevention mechanisms, professional guidance, tested components and speculative technologies must be labeled separately. Success in a tabletop or pilot is not proof of casualty reduction.

    20.1 Defensive process

    Observe & corroborate → classify urgency → choose the least harmful effective safeguard → verify human authority → test the control → record results → improve or withdraw. Do not equate lawful firearm ownership, an identity category, protected expression or an AI score with an individualized threat.

    20.2 Scenario-based protective requirements

    T01 · Household firearm accessible to a minor

    Observable condition: Caregiver identifies an unlocked firearm or accessible key/code. Defensive control: Voluntary locked and unloaded storage guidance; protect combinations; discounted safe or lock referral. Owner: Community firearm-safety lead. Test: Anonymous opt-in adoption survey, lock voucher accounting, and caregiver comprehension exercise. Stop: Ownership reporting required or security intervention based solely on ownership.

    T02 · Firearm in relatives’, friends’, or shared-care locations

    Observable condition: Caregiver concern about access during visits or custody transitions. Defensive control: Universal caregiver discussion guide; voluntary peer-to-peer secure-storage conversation; translated materials. Owner: Family engagement coordinator. Test: Usability test with volunteer caregivers across household types. Stop: Program conditions school access on parental ownership disclosure.

    T03 · Unsecured firearm left in a vehicle

    Observable condition: Voluntary report that firearm was previously left accessible in a vehicle. Defensive control: Avoid unattended vehicle storage; voluntary locked storage education; lawful temporary storage referrals. Owner: Community retailer and caregiver network. Test: Anonymized scenario questionnaire and program distribution audit. Stop: Law-enforcement vehicle search derived from education enrollment data.

    T04 · Credible communicated threat or planning leakage

    Observable condition: Specific observed statements or behavior with corroborating context. Defensive control: Multidisciplinary, behavior-based assessment; documented corroboration; supportive interventions; emergency referral only where justified. Owner: School threat assessment chair. Test: Tabletop case review with synthetic facts and independent review of proportionality. Stop: Unsupported coercion or unsupported record transfer.

    T05 · Malicious, mistaken, or uncorroborated accusation

    Observable condition: Anonymous allegation lacking independently corroborated conduct. Defensive control: Separate intake from finding; preserve exculpatory information; corrective review, time limits and appeals. Owner: Civil rights officer / independent reviewer. Test: Synthetic false-report exercise with review by legal and student representatives. Stop: Student punishment, political profiling or irreversible label without support.

    T06 · Suspected weapon or violence near school arrival/access area

    Observable condition: Direct human report of potential weapon or danger, not algorithmic inference alone. Defensive control: Trained human confirmation where safe; dispatch escalation; campus-specific protective action; accessible notification. Owner: School incident commander / 911 dispatch. Test: No-live-weapon notification tabletop; measure decision and alert chain. Stop: Protective action depends entirely on a network sensor or unvalidated classifier.

    T07 · False or missed acoustic/visual firearm alarm

    Observable condition: Sensor activates without verified incident, or misses simulated signal. Defensive control: Independent sensor benchmark; source provenance; human verification; alternate reporting channel. Owner: Technology assurance lead. Test: Controlled authorized synthetic tests, blinded false-positive and false-negative assessments. Stop: Vendor cannot disclose validation data; false alerts trigger irreversible action.

    T08 · Threat reported while groups are in hallways or common areas

    Observable condition: Verified immediate threat with mixed locations and conflicting movement instructions. Defensive control: Preplanned options-based training; intelligible multi-channel guidance; trained staff judgement. Owner: Emergency preparedness lead. Test: Accessible, non-violent exercise with multiple mobility profiles and observers. Stop: Single inflexible command unnecessarily exposes or traps occupants.

    T09 · Classroom door hardware fails or impedes egress

    Observable condition: Routine inspection finds fault, incompatible accessory, or emergency release failure. Defensive control: Code-reviewed listed hardware; scheduled inspection; manual opening and release; accessibility testing. Owner: Facilities / fire code authority. Test: Qualified walk-through under power-off and simulated panic conditions. Stop: Any tested exit obstruction or inability to release from inside.

    T10 · Person with disability cannot perceive or follow protective instruction

    Observable condition: Audible-only alert or obstructed accessible exit. Defensive control: Multimodal accessible alerts, individualized support planning, accessible refuge/egress review. Owner: ADA/504 coordinator. Test: Inclusive drill with disabled participants and voluntary accommodations. Stop: Protective protocol excludes or isolates disabled occupants.

    T11 · Power or communications outage during incident

    Observable condition: Loss of power, WAN, cellular, or security control unit. Defensive control: Tested backup power; manual public address / radio alternatives; fail-safe access and written procedures. Owner: Facilities / IT / incident command. Test: Authorized loss-of-power functional test with observer and fire authority. Stop: Any power loss locks an occupied exit or disables all emergency notification.

    T12 · Spoofed alert, ransomware, or compromised access-control system

    Observable condition: Unexpected vendor login, unsigned command, inconsistent emergency message. Defensive control: MFA; least privilege; segmentation; signed updates; manual override; offline incident playbook. Owner: CISO / physical security owner. Test: Authorized tabletop and isolated cyber-physical failure injection (no live student impact). Stop: Remote operator can close all egress or issue unverified lethal-threat instructions.

    T13 · Life-threatening bleeding before EMS arrival

    Observable condition: Observed injury requiring immediate emergency medical response. Defensive control: Bleeding-control kits, trained personnel, accessible cache locations, EMS handoff coordination. Owner: School nurse / EMS liaison. Test: Skills-based medical training and equipment inspection; coordinate with qualified clinicians. Stop: Kits inaccessible or training not sustained.

    T14 · Delayed police arrival during an active threat

    Observable condition: Dispatch acknowledges incident but units are not yet on scene. Defensive control: Protective actions, verified alarms, trained communications lead, EMS staging; only legally authorized professional security with oversight. Owner: District emergency lead / law enforcement agency. Test: Multi-agency tabletop; timed non-weapon exercise; authority and ID checks. Stop: Unqualified staff armed or force authority delegated to software.

    T15 · Canine false indication, injury, or unreliable deployment

    Observable condition: Canine proposed for explosives/firearms screening or a positive indication occurs. Defensive control: Qualified canine/handler credentialing; validated application-specific reliability; welfare and student safety review. Owner: Canine program supervisor / independent evaluator. Test: Double-blind controlled screening trials; documented incident review. Stop: Insufficient canine validation or foreseeable student bite/exposure risk.

    T16 · Misidentified person or unauthorized on-site use of force

    Observable condition: Confusing observations; responder has incomplete incident picture. Defensive control: Qualified law-enforcement coordination; identification and restraint standards; independent force review; medical response. Owner: Authorized law enforcement / independent oversight. Test: Legal policy review, credentials audit and scenario-based decision exercise without weapons. Stop: Unlawful force policy; missing chain of command or independent review.

    T17 · AI-generated accusation, synthetic media, or automated threat score

    Observable condition: Suspicious synthetic audio/video or automated recommendation presents urgent allegation. Defensive control: Source verification, human case review, multiple independent observations and challenge process. Owner: AI assurance officer / threat team. Test: Adversarial synthetic exercise, subgroup error analysis and documentation review. Stop: Model recommendation is treated as proof or enables automatic police action.

    T18 · After-event reunification confusion or sensitive-data leakage

    Observable condition: Families receive contradictory or over-detailed status messages. Defensive control: Single authorized family information lead, secure verification, accessible multilingual updates. Owner: Reunification coordinator / privacy officer. Test: Family-facing tabletop, multilingual review, redacted communication audit. Stop: Live roster exposed or misinformation uncontrolled.

    20.3 Five cross-cutting qualification gates

    1. Necessity: identify a documented threat, exposed population and unmet protective gap; consider low-tech alternatives.
    2. Protection: demonstrate plausible benefit and measure alert delay, errors, accessibility and secondary hazards.
    3. Rights: document lawful authority, FERPA restrictions, nondiscrimination, due process, community input and complaint mechanisms.
    4. Cyber-resilience: validate vendor access, updates, segmentation, identity controls, offline fallback and incident response.
    5. Independent acceptance: a board-designated evaluator documents objective evidence and approves, restricts, suspends or rejects the capability.

    20.4 Release and decision authority

    Publish only de-identified scenarios and aggregated validation outcomes. Withhold detailed campus maps, physical bypass or penetration methods, school-specific timing diagrams, sensitive credentials, identities of threat reporters and student case materials. A district safety director cannot override a fire authority's egress determination; procurement staff cannot waive privacy/constitutional gates; AI and sensors cannot authorize physical force.
